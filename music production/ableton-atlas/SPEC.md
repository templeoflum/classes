# Ableton Atlas

## Product and teaching specification

Draft 1.0 · September 20, 2026 · Atlas frame proposal and software-fluency emphasis

**Thirty-minute private Ableton lessons, supported by teacher-written lesson notes and an optional Atlas for revisiting ideas, exploring further, and finding useful musical vocabulary.**

The lesson is the complete core experience. The student should be able to return without having opened any provided material; everything needed to continue is re-established together.

Start with the [Atlas content outline](</Users/donovan/work/classes/music production/ableton-atlas/ATLAS_OUTLINE.md>). Companion documents: [Curriculum and examples](</Users/donovan/work/classes/music production/ableton-atlas/CURRICULUM.md>) · [Private source review](</Users/donovan/work/classes/music production/ableton-atlas/SOURCE_REVIEW.md>) · [Review coverage](</Users/donovan/work/classes/music production/ableton-atlas/SOURCE_CATALOG.md>).

The source-grounded design from draft 0.3 remains, but this revision corrects its assumed usage: notes are primarily written by the teacher during lessons, not maintained by the student between them. The Atlas and student additions are optional extensions. Thirty minutes is the confirmed lesson length, not a provisional hour. Research coverage and remaining gaps remain explicit in the companion documents.

This revision records the first learner's software-fluency emphasis, complete shortcut/device reference requirements, and a revised six-view frame in the outline. View names and distribution remain proposed; the existing local interface has not been rebuilt to match them. Content relationships precede another surface pass or demo production. No application or student materials have been released.

## 1. What is settled, and what is proposed

Settled by our conversations:

- Create our own teaching voice, examples, musical material, and organization. Study other courses for patterns, not text or a sequence to reproduce.
- Start with private lessons for an experienced piano teacher who is new to Live. Respect her musicianship while building software fluency from scratch.
- Treat her existing musical judgment as the starting point. Emphasize production vocabulary, device knowledge, and movement through Live; do not assume she needs instruction in songwriting, contrast, or composition.
- Make shortcuts a habit from the beginning: recall or look up an action, perform it in Live, and reuse it inside small musical tasks during the lesson.
- Provide every documented built-in shortcut and every device within a declared supported Live/edition/Pack inventory, with contexts, uses, and availability. Comprehensive reference does not mean comprehensive memorization or an exhaustive opening lesson.
- Plan for 30-minute lessons that work with zero between-lesson use of the materials.
- The teacher normally writes brief notes during the lesson. The student can refer back or expand on them if she wishes; neither is expected.
- Make an Ableton Atlas: a place to browse, experiment, remember, and return with questions.
- Keep between-lesson exploration light and optional. No prescribed homework pipeline.
- Use one connected body of knowledge. No Pianist/Producer/Beatmaker identity paths or suggested-path selector.
- Use lesson notes for continuity; offer questions, revisiting, deeper exploration, and vocabulary without requiring student participation in a tracker.
- Support future focused classes without rebuilding the whole content library.
- Offer future demo projects individually through their Atlas concepts, letting a collection grow through choice rather than delivering every file at once.
- Establish the Atlas outline before designing its surface or producing the demo library.
- Make the map the main workspace: doing, locating, connecting, plotting a personal route, and seeing deliberately kept history. Explanations sit beneath those interactions, not in front of them.
- Make connections and available routes visible without telling the reader what to think, hear, feel, or try next. The Atlas is not a narrator; interpretation belongs to the learner and the lesson.
- Make people, aims, decisions, and documented lineages part of the map itself. Tools are responses to particular intentions and conditions, not a self-explanatory progression of features.
- Begin alongside the actual first opening of Live, close to the learner's situation. Do not require an Atlas orientation or reveal territory through completion gates.
- Distinguish explored together, temporary browsing history, and wanting to return. None measures mastery; no automatic persistent activity tracking.

Proposals in this draft, still adjustable:

- A primary map with contextual access to lesson notes and optional detail. These are connected kinds of information, not a commitment to two equal tabs or a separate student progress dashboard.
- Six distinct views: Studio, Actions, Devices, Sound & Form, Approaches, and Lineages. The fourteen coverage areas and earlier 42 concept IDs remain an authoring inventory; they do not cap shortcut/device coverage. The earlier 15-entry pilot pool is a resource, not a fixed release list. No count is a learner target or a commitment to six identical diagrams.
- Ten flexible teaching encounters rather than a ten-week commitment.
- Start with a teacher-maintained lesson record and a readable copy made available to the student. The delivery/editing medium is undecided; a student-only browser notebook is no longer the default architecture.
- Live 12 as the initial authoring baseline, subject to the actual student's version and edition.

The intended reference is [The Piano, Mapped](https://templeoflum.github.io/piano-atlas/), with its [public source](https://github.com/templeoflum/piano-atlas). The local checkout remains unlocated; that does not leave the reference unidentified. The chord viewer and `pianoatlas.app` are different projects. We adapt the organizing principles, not their code, text, or visual design.

## 2. Purpose and success

The first outcome is independent musical agency in Live: the student can get sound working, keep an idea, make an intentional change, compare the result, and return to the work later.

Over time, she should be able to:

- Distinguish a musical decision from the software action that carries it out.
- Follow a simple signal path when something is silent or unexpected.
- Capture a performance and decide which aspects to preserve or edit.
- Develop material through sound, rhythm, layering, form, and space.
- Save and find work, and make a listening copy when she wants one.
- Recognize a useful question and bring it into the next conversation.

These are teacher-observed capabilities, not a scored checklist displayed to the student. A satisfying sketch, a useful sound, a recorded improvisation, or a clearer question can all be worthwhile outcomes. A finished release is not the default requirement.

The design succeeds even when the student never reads a note, follows a link, marks a concept, or experiments between lessons. Progress may happen entirely in the time together. Optional materials make further engagement possible; they are not a dependency for the next lesson.

## 3. Teaching philosophy

### Musical intention leads

Introduce a tool in response to a musical possibility: keeping a phrase, changing its articulation, making room for another part, or hearing a different ending. Interface navigation supports those actions. An exhaustive interface tour is not the opening lesson.

### Existing musicianship is an asset

Use her hearing, phrasing, touch, voicing, and teaching experience as a bridge. Do not assume that knowing music implies knowing signal flow, MIDI, monitoring, or file management. Equally, do not spend time reteaching scales or harmony unless she asks.

The teacher now describes her as professionally trained, with an impeccable ear and taste. The practical success measure is less friction between musical intention and an audible result, not improvement against our aesthetic preferences. Her reported aspirational references are *Headlock* by Imogen Heap and *Lost Weekend* by Phoebe Bridgers; specific moments should be discussed together before choosing a technical emphasis. This records the teacher's report, not a completed analysis of either recording.

Piano is an entry point, not a boundary. The material should leave room for unfamiliar instruments, recorded textures, rhythm, and different kinds of music.

### Software technique is core musical practice

Introduce useful shortcuts from the first lesson, especially finding and loading what is wanted, navigating to the relevant object, editing, undoing, comparing, and saving. If a shortcut is forgotten, look it up and use it in Live. Repetition comes through meaningful work during the half-hour, not tests or assumed homework. Mouse use is not a failure; teach the movement that serves the action and the learner's access needs.

For the first navigation examples, prioritize Tab (Session/Arrangement), Shift–Tab (Clip/Device), and hiding/showing Live's Browser over numbered focus-jump shortcuts. The documented Browser toggle is Command–Option–B on Mac and Ctrl–Alt–B on Windows. Tab behavior depends on context and the “Use Tab to Move Focus” setting; record that qualification alongside the shortcut rather than silently assuming one setup.

The Atlas supports fast action/device lookup with selection, focus, version, OS, and edition context. It neither operates Live nor intercepts the learner's Live commands. Teach a few useful movements at a time while keeping the full reference reachable.

### Demonstrate a decision, then return control

A useful teaching cycle is: hear an intention, show one change, let the student try, compare, discuss. The teacher models uncertainty and listening as well as successful operations. The student spends substantial time controlling Live herself.

### Preserve alternatives

Show how to keep an original before changing it. Compare versions without naming one “correct.” Quantizing, leaving timing alone, changing an instrument, and removing an effect are all choices with consequences.

### Focus attention without separating the music into jobs

It can help to focus on one question for a while: what should lead, how the phrase should arrive, or whether a passage needs less happening. But sound, performance, form, and balance affect one another. A changed envelope may invite different notes; a clearer arrangement may make an EQ adjustment unnecessary. Follow that relationship when it becomes audible. Do not require the learner to choose a role, mode, or fixed production stage.

A small, temporary constraint can make a choice easier to hear. Explain its purpose, offer it rather than impose it, and release it when it stops helping. No sketch quotas, time-box contracts, or prescribed track ingredients carry over from the research sources.

### Keep discoveries without requiring finished pieces

A phrase, a responsive sound, a useful comparison, or a question can be kept on its own. Saving an idea does not create an obligation to develop it. Reuse and subtraction are creative possibilities alongside adding new material. Personal taste emerges through these decisions; it need not be defined by an artist archetype or branding exercise first.

### Revisit at greater depth

Timing, signal flow, sound shape, and listening recur in different musical situations. A first encounter should be useful without explaining every related mechanism. Later detail connects to something already heard.

### Leave room for a life outside lessons

Plan each next lesson assuming no materials were used. Reintroduce relevant ideas through sound and action, using the teacher's own record for continuity. If the student brings an experiment or question, welcome it and adapt. No catch-up debt accumulates, and there is no need to explain non-use.

An invitation need not be issued after every lesson. It can simply remain available beside a relevant note. Learning a term may help the student ask a more specific question, but everyday descriptions, gestures, and playing remain equally welcome.

## 4. The three connected parts

| Part | Main purpose | What it contains |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Private lesson | The complete core learning experience | Thirty minutes of playing, listening, demonstration, troubleshooting, and conversation |
| Lesson notes | Teacher-maintained continuity, available to the student | Brief records of what happened, useful terms, kept ideas, questions, and optional links |
| Atlas | Optional embodied map, reference, vocabulary, and discovery | Connected musical situations, actions, original examples, optional depth, and deliberately kept places |

The teacher carries continuity whether or not the student uses the notes. The Atlas can deepen a conversation but is never preparation required to take part in one.

## 5. Atlas organization

The [content outline](</Users/donovan/work/classes/music production/ableton-atlas/ATLAS_OUTLINE.md>) is the current organizational proposal: **Studio** maps the working system; **Actions** maps operations and shortcuts; **Devices** maps mechanisms, controls, and uses; **Sound & Form** maps musical relationships at different scales; **Approaches** maps aims, choices, and tradeoffs; **Lineages** maps documented human and technical histories. This supersedes the earlier six-territory distribution. The fourteen-area inventory checks subject coverage underneath; shortcut/device catalogs extend beyond the existing concept IDs.

These are different views of shared entries, not levels. A concept has one stable identity and can appear in several meaningful contexts without duplicating its explanation. Studio's practical relationships survive; Sound and Phrase become different scales within Sound & Form. No obligation to preserve the old three tabs or duplicate content into persona courses.

People, collaborators, scenes, aims, constraints, decisions, tools, and works are first-class connected places—not a history appendix. A useful historical branch identifies what a person or group pursued, which convention or limitation applied in that setting, what changed, and how a practice was transmitted, adapted, disputed, or independently approached elsewhere. No obligation to supply a founder or rebellion for every concept.

Approaches and Lineages have their own substantial frames, not just attached biographies or repeated person/aim/tool mini-diagrams. The former compares purposes and methods; the latter shows documented relationships over time. Both cross-link to relevant practical and musical entries. Each view's structure follows its relationships rather than a universal diagram template.

**Initial historical scope:** center both views on *Headlock*, *Lost Weekend* (the title track), and Ableton Live’s origins. Begin with the people, source recordings, contributions, needs, and choices actually connected to those anchors. The earlier Oram/Reich examples are retained as research, not displayed as disconnected starting points. Further history enters through a documented connection to a record, working practice, or software feature already in view. Distinguish track credits, album-level accounts, listening observations, and proposed Live studies. A credit does not establish a person’s intent; a similar device does not establish the original signal chain. The first revision maps credits and written accounts; a time-coded listening breakdown is not yet complete.

Distinguish documented collaboration, teaching, influence, adaptation, and explicit disagreement from an editorial comparison. Shared tools or proximity in a diagram do not prove descent. Attribute aims to a source; label design-based interpretations as summaries rather than quotations. Scope a claimed convention to a time and community instead of declaring one universal standard. Include supporting labor, institutions, and communities; avoid a lone-genius genealogy or a claim that everything culminates in Ableton. Explanations and evidence remain optional depth, while names and relationships are visible on the map.

Not every entry teaches a feature. “Compare two possibilities,” “make room,” and “keep this fragment” sit alongside recording, envelopes, and file care. These practices are useful from the beginning without requiring advanced production tools. The map does not need equal numbers of entries in each neighborhood.

Search should accept both terminology and ordinary language. Examples: “keyboard is silent,” “keep my timing,” “sound fades in,” “why is the other clip playing?” Editorial search synonyms are sufficient for the pilot; no conversational AI dependency is needed.

Entries should bridge ordinary language and useful vocabulary: “the sound takes a while to arrive” can connect to attack. A term should help someone recognize a sound or express a question, not become a word they must memorize before asking for help.

Useful connections have a stated reason:

- **Helpful context:** signal flow helps diagnose silent MIDI playback.
- **Compare:** note length and envelope release affect different parts of articulation.
- **Related possibility:** a captured performance can become an audio texture.

Connections never lock a page or prescribe the student's next task. The map is the primary workspace: position, proximity, connection labels, and a clear way back should communicate useful relationships before explanatory reading. Exact geometry and styling remain open; decorative complexity and a miniature recreation of Live are not goals. Provide an equivalent plain-language, keyboard-accessible route through the same relationships. Keep origins, alternatives, and limitations available beside explanations.

Open close to “I've just opened Live,” with a few recognizable nearby actions. The wider map is available immediately. Choosing one's own saved places or route is different from being offered a recommended curriculum. Route editing can follow the first prototype; it is not a requirement to begin.

An optional curiosity flag means “something to return to,” not “completed,” “behind,” or “assigned.” The teacher can record that interest in the lesson note without requiring the student to use the Atlas. Flagging does not require choosing a next path, downloading a project, or maintaining a separate tracker. Teacher notes remain the continuity record; we are not adopting the source site's local-browser storage as a shared-note architecture.

## 6. What a concept entry contains

Every published entry should be independently useful and small enough to revisit during music-making.

The ingredients below describe concept writing, not one compulsory format for all content. Shortcut entries need exact action/focus/key context; devices need signal role, mechanism, controls, uses, and availability; people and practices need contextual sources and typed relationships. An original audio example is not required for every shortcut or historical entry. Full shortcut and device inventory requirements are specified in the outline.

1. **An identifiable place.** A concept, material, practice, or question someone can locate without adopting a prescribed interpretation.
2. **A short explanation.** Plain language first; precise terms where they help.
3. **One original example.** A brief demonstration, comparison, annotated screenshot, or original Live Set excerpt. Use only the media that clarifies this particular concept.
4. **A foothold in Live.** Enough orientation to try it in the supported version, with edition requirements and an alternative when needed.
5. **Optional exploration material, when useful.** An example or experiment may be available at depth, not issued as the default voice of every entry. No required number of bars, genres, uploads, or finished products.
6. **A way back.** How to stop, undo, restore the reference, or recover from the common confusion.
7. **A few purposeful connections.** Usually two to four, with reasons.
8. **Optional response.** Offer a lightweight way to keep a question or add a thought where the chosen medium supports it. Reading alone is a complete use; no response control is needed to teach from the entry.

The first visible layer is a recognizable place, its labeled connections, and a way back. Do not pre-interpret the experience with poetic conclusions, “notice this,” or “try that.” Names, position, and relation labels make navigation possible; definitions, examples, and technical explanations are available on request. Signal paths, temporal relationships, and conceptual connections must not masquerade as a recommended learning sequence. The existing 150–300-word examples belong to optional reference detail, not an obligatory front-page reading panel. These entry ingredients are an authoring checklist, not eight sections every visitor must read. Screenshots and operation instructions stay separate from durable musical explanations.

**Surface-copy rule:** show names, relationship labels, controls, and operation-critical context. Do not add a narrator around the map: no explanatory taglines, repeated category/caption stacks, “frame study” badges, sample-count footers, “not a simulator” disclaimers, or announcements about future authoring. Build status belongs on the private board. Scope and evidence limits remain available in collapsed About/Sources; storage limitations remain visible where a person relies on them. Notes that are examples must still be labelled as examples. This is a standing editorial constraint, not a one-time cosmetic cleanup.

A card must not become a miniature textbook chapter. Nor should it promise a demo or download that has not been authored and checked.

## 7. Teacher-written lesson notes

### Default authorship and purpose

The teacher jots brief, student-readable notes during the lesson, preferably at natural pauses while listening, comparing, or saving. Notes should support attention, not compete with playing. They do not need to become polished reports, and the student does not need to dictate, approve, or complete a reflection form.

A useful record might remember:

- What we played, tried, or noticed.
- A useful term beside its ordinary-language meaning.
- Where the idea was saved or how to return to it.
- A question to revisit together.
- An optional Atlas link when it adds something specific.

These are possibilities, not required fields. A dated sentence or a few bullets may be enough. Link only the most relevant resources; do not turn each lesson into a reading bundle. The notes provide a lightweight history of what has been explored without claiming that a topic was mastered.

### Example of the intended scale

> Compared the same chord with a quick and slower entrance. You left more space with the slower sound. “Attack” is the time the sound takes to rise at its beginning. Saved both versions in Evening sketch. We can return to how the sustain pedal affects the ending. Optional: Let a sound arrive and leave.

This is an illustrative note, not a record of a lesson that has occurred. It includes a word she can use later, but understanding that word is not a condition for returning.

### Optional student additions

The student can read, annotate, add a question, or expand a note if she wants. A correction or addition can also be spoken next lesson; using the companion is never the only route. Examples: “Does attack also change the tone?” or “I found another sound that makes me play more slowly.”

Keep teacher notes and student additions distinguishable when both exist. Do not silently replace one person's words with another's. A simple pilot can use student annotations on a copy or additions relayed in conversation; collaborative editing is not promised until a suitable medium is selected.

### Progress and privacy

The lesson record supplies the initial progression history. A teacher may deliberately mark a concept as explored together; the learner or teacher may keep a place to revisit. Navigation history is temporary by default, not a persistent record of engagement. These meanings stay distinct. Do not infer ability, engagement, or regression from visits, downloads, or lack of notes. A discussion mark is not the student's self-assessment of understanding.

No mandatory reflection forms, confidence scores, streaks, grades, progress percentages, overdue labels, or engagement monitoring. Student-readable lesson notes are separate from private teacher preparation or evaluative notes. Private preparation is never included in a shared recap by default.

Keep music in Live and the student's own files. Notes may point to a recognizable name or location without uploading or analyzing the Project.

## 8. Main experiences

### First opening: enter where Live is

Start at the learner's actual situation: play and hear, recover a missing sound, or enter through an existing musical idea. A small nearby map connects playing, keeping an idea, and changing a sound's response. None must be completed in order. The Atlas does not detect or control Live. The [first-opening blueprint](</Users/donovan/work/classes/music production/ableton-atlas/FIRST_OPENING_BLUEPRINT.md>) defines this behavior and an honest setup-only stopping point.

### During the lesson: the default workflow

The teacher briefly recalls the last useful starting point, hears what matters today, and follows one manageable musical question with the student. The teacher captures a few notes as they work. They save what is worth keeping. The record is made available for reference without requiring any follow-up action.

### Lesson Notes

The student can find dated, readable lesson records and relevant Atlas links. No empty notebook waiting for her to populate it. Where supported, she can add a thought or question; otherwise she can annotate a copy or bring it up in conversation. She is not responsible for keeping the lesson history current.

### Explore the Atlas

Move through connected places when something catches her interest, with search as another way in. Hear or try something, connect an everyday description to a term, and open explanation if useful. Keep, rearrange, or abandon a personal route without a recommended-path selector. No route creation, progress registration, or prescribed reading is required. An entry can also support a demonstration during a lesson.

### Between lessons: no use is the planning baseline

She does not open the materials or work in Live. At the next lesson, the teacher uses their record and a short musical reminder to resume or start elsewhere. Nothing is missing, overdue, or awaiting submission.

### Between lessons: an optional deeper visit

She rereads the note about a slower entrance, follows the envelope link, and finds the word “attack.” She may try something, add a question, or simply ask about it next time. Each of those is useful; none is necessary to justify access to the resource.

### A return after a gap

The teacher can locate the last idea and re-establish context together. The student need not retrieve it unaided or demonstrate that she studied the notes. No inactivity reminders or assumed regression.

## 9. First-student teaching model

Before preparing device-specific material, establish her Live version/edition, computer, keyboard connection, audio output, and whether she wants to record acoustic piano, play software instruments, compose, perform, or something else. Those are current interests, not permanent identities.

Each lesson is 30 minutes. A useful planning rhythm is about three minutes to reconnect, twenty-two minutes with one main musical question, and five minutes to save and establish a return point. Notes are jotted during the work; do not reserve a large separate reporting block. These times are guides, not targets to hit.

The proposed first encounter is to play a responsive sound, capture a short idea, listen, and save/reopen it where time permits. If setup takes the session, a working sound and a documented way to return can be enough; recording or editing can wait. Do not compress an hour-long plan into the half hour.

Arrangement is a reasonable starting proposal for recording a continuous performance. Session can come first if launching and combining phrases better matches her immediate interest. Explain both as uses of one Set when the distinction becomes relevant; neither is reserved for a particular kind of musician.

The [curriculum](</Users/donovan/work/classes/music production/ableton-atlas/CURRICULUM.md>) supplies ten reusable encounters, detailed first-session outlines, and five sample Atlas entries. They are planning resources for the teacher, not a learner-facing required path. Timing and order adapt to the student.

Aim for a small, complete creative experience early: play, keep, hear a possible change, choose, and find the result again. This is not a mandatory sequence inside every lesson. The point is that musical agency begins before interface fluency is complete.

When responding to her work, first ask what matters to her, identify an audible moment, and offer a comparison or a few possible responses. Let her choose what to keep. Distinguish a technical failure, such as no output, from an aesthetic preference, such as wanting a gentler entrance. If useful, remember one unresolved question together.

## 10. Original materials to produce

### Student-facing

- Concept entries and short, original demonstrations.
- A compact personal setup/reference sheet using her actual configuration.
- Optional exploration invitations embedded in the relevant entries.
- Brief teacher-written lesson notes, available for optional reference and additions.
- Original example Sets and audio comparisons with clear compatibility notes.

### Teacher-facing

- A session guide: musical intention, preparation, short demonstration, likely confusion, simplification, possible extension, and a natural stopping point.
- Source/research notes kept separate from publishable content.
- A technical verification and asset-permission record.
- Notes from the pilot identifying which explanations helped and where the student needed a different entry point.

Slide decks and polished printable workbooks are later packaging options, not prerequisites to teaching the first lessons. Author the teaching content first.

### Demo principles

Use our own phrases, performances, recordings, patches, and screenshots. Keep an untouched reference; label changes specifically. Level-match comparisons where loudness is a confound. Start playback only on user action, provide stop/volume controls, and avoid sudden loud examples.

A demonstration should expose a decision, not display the largest possible device chain. An ordinary phrase heard clearly is more useful than a complex finished track whose changes are difficult to isolate.

### Concept-linked demo projects

Demo titles are currently sketches, not files to generate yet. Let the agreed Atlas outline determine their purpose. A ready project is offered individually from the concept entry that explains it; related entries may reference the same project. No default bulk pack, timed unlocks, completion gates, or obligation to build a collection.

Each future download should explain its question, starting action, way back, tested version/edition, and included dependencies. The student can retain the original and keep personal variations. A teacher can use it during the lesson without prior student downloading; text/audio alternatives keep the concept useful without a Set. Downloads do not become evidence of learning or engagement.

## 11. Authorship and source boundaries

Our editorial method is: identify a general teaching pattern, explain why it serves this student's goal, design an original example, then validate it independently.

Do not reproduce source prose, slide layouts, diagrams, assignment recipes, distinctive analogies, branded frameworks, course packets, recordings, or prescribed sequencing. Do not redistribute source presets, samples, or Live Sets. Having a file locally does not establish permission to include its contents in our own materials.

A source note records influence and uncertainty; it is not permission to copy. When an external resource is genuinely useful for further reading, link to an appropriate authorized page. Original learner material should stand on its own without access to a paid source course.

Keep the research documents and source paths outside any student-site publishing input. Do not deploy this whole project directory indiscriminately.

## 12. Technical accuracy and compatibility

Treat the historical Live 9 material, mixed-version EMA notes, and saved Synthesis workflows as pedagogical research, not a current operating manual. Teacher packets, personal shorthand, and AI meeting summaries have different evidentiary weight. Verify exact operations, shortcuts, edition availability, and terminology against the chosen Live version and a working installation before student release.

Important editorial safeguards:

- Musical preferences and workflow defaults must not be presented as universal technical laws.
- Preserve expression when teaching timing and dynamics; editing is optional.
- Separate audio from MIDI, note velocity from track gain, note duration from envelope behavior, and clip automation from relative modulation.
- Distinguish monitoring delay from recorded alignment and MIDI clock timing. Do not prescribe Driver Error Compensation as a general cure for sluggish playing.
- Treat a listening export, a reusable Project, and a delivery/mastering package as different things.
- Never imply that collecting files bundles third-party plugin installations or grants redistribution rights.
- Label hardware-dependent and edition-dependent examples before the student tries them.
- Check the state and routing behind device claims; a sidechain connection alone does not specify whether something opens, closes, or ducks.
- Separate a sound's source, playback behavior, processing, and musical role without making that distinction a prerequisite to experimentation.

The [source review](</Users/donovan/work/classes/music production/ableton-atlas/SOURCE_REVIEW.md>) identifies specific historical explanations requiring correction, with current Ableton references. New content receives its own technical check even where a source sounds authoritative.

## 13. Proposed companion MVP

This describes the intended first product beyond the initial [local interaction sketch](</Users/donovan/work/classes/music production/ableton-atlas/workbench/README.md>). The sketch now tests one neighborhood, recovery, and a fictional return note; it is not a student-ready companion or real note system. Custom software is not necessary to deliver the lessons.

### In scope

- A low-friction way for the teacher to write and revisit dated lesson notes during a 30-minute session.
- A readable student-facing copy of those notes, with selected links to Atlas entries.
- An optional way to add a question or annotation, using the chosen medium; never required for lesson continuity.
- A legible local map, a wider view, stable links, purposeful connections, search, and an equivalent non-visual navigation route.
- Lightweight deliberate return markers and lesson-linked encounters, with temporary back-navigation history. No automatic persistent activity log or mastery inference.
- Original audio comparisons with accessible controls and text descriptions.
- Individual demo-project downloads in the relevant concept context, only after the original files are authored and checked; no bulk distribution or unlock mechanism.
- Clear authorship, intended audience, and recovery/backup behavior for notes.
- Keyboard-accessible controls, visible focus, sufficient contrast, and no color-only meanings.

The map is the primary surface; reference detail and lesson context support it. Do not make a learner scorecard or management dashboard the landing experience. An optional question stays beside its context. The private project dashboard used to coordinate this build is a separate authoring aid, not a student product feature.

### Out of scope initially

Custom real-time co-editing, custom accounts, automatic persistent activity tracking, messaging infrastructure, billing, scheduling, grades, certificates, required submissions, AI coaching, automatic Ableton analysis, live DAW control, media uploads, and social/community features. Sophisticated personal-route editing and inferred familiarity are not part of the initial prototype.

Browser playback illustrates concepts; it is not a recreation of Live. Project references are labels or links, not a promise to open or interpret arbitrary .als files.

### Delivery, storage, and privacy

The previous student-only, browser-local notebook proposal is withdrawn as the default: it does not by itself make teacher-written notes available to the student.

For a simple pilot, the teacher can retain the dated record and deliberately provide a readable copy through an agreed channel. The student should not need to perform an import, submit a note, or install a custom app to receive the recap. Student additions can be made on a copy or brought to the next lesson. This does not imply automatic synchronization or two-way editing.

Choose the actual document medium, access controls, and sharing method before delivery. If a shared document service is used, check the recipient and permissions; do not publish individual lesson notes with the public Atlas. A private-looking URL is not authentication. Any future custom shared system needs an appropriate authenticated design before implementation.

Keep student-readable notes and private teacher preparation separate. Preview any shared export so it excludes other students' records and private preparation. Preserve originals and author attribution when incorporating additions. Provide a recoverable record and a way to obtain a readable copy; never silently overwrite newer material. The exact backup and editing mechanics depend on the selected medium.

No notes have been shared and no service has been selected by this specification.

| Lesson note | Stable reference, lesson date, student association, teacher author, text, intended audience | Teacher-maintained; may link to concepts or a saved idea; no student input required |
| Student addition/question | Author, text, date, optional note/concept reference | Optional; distinguishable from the original note; may originate in conversation |
| Deliberately kept place | Concept reference, marker meaning, author, optional lesson reference | Distinguish explored together from want to return; no mastery inference or automatic page-visit record; storage/access still to be selected |
| Private teacher preparation | Separate teacher-only record | Excluded from the student-facing history and sharing by default |
| Focused class | ID, purpose, selected concept IDs, original facilitation material | Curated teaching, not a new persona or duplicated Atlas |
| Asset register | ID, creator/permission, purpose, version/edition requirements | Unknown permission blocks student distribution |

These are conceptual records, not a requirement to build a database before teaching. Content publication states are draft, technically checked, and ready; they are authoring states, not learner achievement. Concept renaming preserves its ID and note references. Retiring a concept preserves a readable reference for old lesson notes.

## 15. Rollout and acceptance

The [phased build plan](</Users/donovan/work/classes/music production/ableton-atlas/BUILD_PLAN.md>) expands the stages below into deliverables, dependencies, and readiness checks. Use it for execution order. Teaching can begin with a small written subset while the Atlas is built; demo production and publication remain later, separately requested work.

### Stage A: a teachable pilot

The first-opening/return blueprint has become a small local interaction sketch with short entry fronts. Basic navigation checks have been recorded; final visual design, student testing, and demo-file creation remain later work. The earlier three-Set proposal and larger demo list are possibilities to select from later, not parallel production commitments.

For later pilot authoring, use the 15 entries named in the curriculum as a starting pool. Prepare only the subset needed for the first encounter before teaching; do not delay it until every entry is finished. The first three session guides remain useful; a personal setup sheet and a few original demonstrations can follow actual need. Reuse a clear example across related entries when appropriate and provide audio alternatives for incompatible devices. Assets remain planned, not supplied.

The pilot can be taught from simple documents before the browser companion exists. Test brief in-lesson teacher note-taking and making a readable copy available. Teach the following lesson without depending on the student having read it. Ask whether the explanations help before investing in interface polish.

### Stage B: the smallest useful Atlas

Build only what the approved content outline and pilot show is useful. Test the baseline workflow first: teacher writes during the lesson, notes are available, student does nothing between lessons, teacher resumes smoothly. Then test the optional extension: student revisits a note, follows a relevant concept, and perhaps chooses its demo project or brings a question. The existing 42 concepts are a revisable starting inventory, not a required course or the limit of the Atlas.

### Stage C: focused classes

Develop independent classes around a musical question: expressive electronic instruments, turning recordings into playable material, arranging through contrast, performing with Live, or mixing through listening. Reuse stable concepts while writing original class-specific demonstrations and facilitation notes.

### Acceptance checks

- A complete 30-minute lesson works when the student has not opened any materials since the last one.
- The teacher can write a brief useful note without substantially interrupting playing or listening.
- The next lesson can resume from the teacher's record without asking the student to reconstruct or maintain it.
- The student can read the provided notes without having to contribute or mark progress.
- An optional addition remains distinguishable from the teacher's original note.
- A plain-language question is welcome even if the student has learned none of the supplied vocabulary.
- Atlas content remains accessible without a role, tracker, or homework commitment.
- First arrival offers a useful action beside Live without requiring an introduction; wider exploration is available immediately.
- Returning preserves or re-establishes context without repeated onboarding, assumed homework, or a claim that the Atlas knows Live's state.
- Essential connections and back-navigation are understandable without dragging a map or relying on color.
- A learner can choose one ready concept-linked project without receiving the whole library or completing another topic; no download is required to take part in a lesson.
- Sharing excludes private preparation and other students' records; storage and editing expectations are explicit.
- Renaming or revising a concept does not orphan lesson-note references.
- Missing media has a readable fallback; audio never starts unexpectedly.
- Every distributed asset has known permission and tested compatibility.
- The teacher can simplify or revisit an encounter without describing the student as behind.

Evidence of teaching success comes from musical choices and student feedback during lessons, not companion usage. Optional exploration can enrich the work, but zero use is not a failure signal.
