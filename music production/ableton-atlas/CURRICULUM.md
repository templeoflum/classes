## 1. The musical through-line

Begin with something the student can already express. Give her ways to keep it, hear it differently, make a choice, and carry that choice into another context.

A phrase may recur across several lessons because familiarity makes changes easier to hear. It is equally fine to start fresh, work with a new recording, follow an unexpected sound, or spend a lesson answering a question. No single ongoing project is compulsory.

Each lesson is 30 minutes and stands on its own without between-lesson activity. The teacher keeps brief notes during the lesson and uses that record to support the next one. The student can revisit or expand those notes, explore the Atlas, or do none of those things. Plan for no use; welcome whatever she brings.

The Atlas accompanies opening Live, starting from the learner's actual situation and question. Its map offers a place to locate what is happening, with minimal reading and optional deeper explanation. Meaningful spatial relationships let the learner plot a path through her own interests; the teacher does not assign a map route. An optional encounter or curiosity marker records only that encounter or interest, never mastery. Page visits and playback do not automatically track activity. At the next lesson the teacher restores the saved working point and the musical context together with the learner.

Her musical knowledge lets us start with rich listening questions while keeping the software actions simple. “Which voice should feel closer?” is a meaningful question before she knows the names of three different audio effects.

Software fluency and musical decision-making grow together. There is no preliminary block of button lessons that she must finish before making choices. A sound experiment can influence the phrase immediately; a form question can lead to deleting a layer rather than learning another processor. A small discovery is enough to keep, even when it does not become a track.

## 2. The foundational map: 42 entries

Related entries should explain the relationship. For example, `edit-velocity` connects to `play-instruments` because different instruments interpret the same performance differently, and to `balance-levels` because musical emphasis can come from performance or mixing. These are distinct decisions worth comparing.

Keep the other overlaps explicit: `perform-views` belongs canonically to A's explanation of playback and links to D's performed choices. `shape-layers` concerns components of one sound; `build-roles` concerns musical parts sharing a phrase or piece. `hear-levels` concerns a workable listening/output level and clipping; `balance-levels` concerns musical prominence. None is a prerequisite route into the other.

The six added practice entries address a gap exposed by the full coursework review: operation knowledge alone does not help someone decide whether to change, reuse, remove, or keep something. They belong in the same map, not in a separate creativity track. Their homes above are editorial conveniences, not boundaries on use.

The outline also proposes a phrase bridge and a sourced musical-context entry beyond this inventory. They remain candidates without new stable IDs or a claim of checked learner content. Existing written sources support their topic placement; a particular explanation, listening reference, and any factual claim still need the checks recorded in the source review. Unreviewed course media does not count as additional evidence.

## 3. A flexible set of teaching encounters

The numbers make planning easier; they are not weeks or prerequisites. An encounter is a reusable theme, not a checklist to finish in one half-hour. It can recur, combine with another, or be skipped. File care, comfortable listening levels, and student control recur throughout.

| Encounter | Musical question | Possible work together | Optional possibility, only if wanted |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Hear yourself and keep an idea | How do I make this environment respond to me? | Connect, play one instrument, record a phrase, listen, save and reopen | Return to the sound if you feel like playing; a question is enough to bring back |
| 2. Keep the expression | What matters in how I played it? | Compare the original with one note, touch, or timing change | Notice a moment you would keep exactly as played |
| 3. Meet another kind of instrument | How does the sound change what I want to play? | Explore onset and release, then adapt the phrase to the response | Follow one sound that invites a different gesture |
| 4. Make room for another voice | What does a second layer contribute? | Add a contrasting role; compare register, density, and level before processing | Listen for how two parts share attention, in Live or in music you enjoy |
| 5. Let a phrase become a piece | What makes repetition feel intentional? | Create contrast, try combinations, shape a beginning and ending | Try changing the order or leaving something out |
| 6. Turn a recording into material | What could this sound become? | Record something original; trim, play, or stretch it with an untouched comparison | Bring a sound you noticed, a recording, or simply its description |
| 7. Hear a space | Where does this music seem to happen? | Compare dry sound with a small number of spatial choices | Notice a space you like and what makes it feel that way |
| 8. Give a change a shape | What should move while the notes stay the same? | Perform or draw one change; compare it with periodic movement | Trace an imagined change with your hand, voice, words, or Live controls |
| 9. Help the idea read clearly | What do I want someone to hear first? | Compare changes to level, register, density, or processing when they address the current question | Listen away from the lesson setup and notice one thing |
| 10. Keep something meaningful | What would I like to keep, share, or explore next? | Revisit a question, make a listening copy, or prepare a small performance | Choose whether to continue, start something new, or leave it for now |

There is no expectation to perform these invitations, read a recap, or open the Atlas. Invitations need not be delivered at all; they can remain available in a note or relevant entry. None of the following encounters depends on them. Vocabulary offers another way to express a question, not a prerequisite to asking one in ordinary language or through playing.

Across these encounters, keep three habits available without turning them into a worksheet:

- Compare an intended change with something preserved. Ask what changed musically, not only which control moved.
- Try a smaller intervention when useful: fewer notes, a different register, a pause, or a familiar sound used differently. Processing remains a possibility, not an obligation or a last resort.
- Keep a useful fragment and a recognizable return point. Do not make finishing it the price of saving it.

If attention scatters, offer one temporary focus—for example, listen only to how a phrase ends. Explain why that might help and let her drop it. Do not import a source course's quotas, prescribed ingredients, or release timetable.

## 4. Before the first lesson

Ask one musical question: **“What would you love to be able to do with Live that you cannot easily do now?”**

Then establish only what is needed for a workable session:

- Installed Live version and edition; whether saving is enabled.
- Computer/OS and available listening setup.
- Keyboard/controller model and connection, if any; whether it sends MIDI or audio.
- Whether she means recording the acoustic instrument, using a digital piano's own sound, or playing a software instrument.
- Confirm the practical setup for the 30-minute lesson and whether she can operate it herself.

Prepare one compatible, responsive sound and a clean fallback Set. Do not require a new purchase before testing what she already has. A computer keyboard or drawn MIDI can demonstrate an idea when necessary, but acknowledge that it does not reproduce her piano touch.

Prepare a safe recording setup: modest output, headphones where an open microphone could feed back, a visible stop action, and no unexpected input routing. Diagnose sluggish response before interpreting it as a playing problem. Wired monitoring, buffer settings, and processing load are relevant; their exact setup is device-dependent. See [Ableton's latency guidance](https://help.ableton.com/hc/en-us/articles/209072289-How-to-reduce-latency).

## 5. First three lesson guides

These are 30-minute sessions: approximately three minutes to reconnect, twenty-two minutes of exploration, and five minutes to save and establish a return point. Adjust the scope rather than rush. The teacher jots brief notes at natural pauses during the work; note-taking should not displace the student's playing. Do not expect any preparation or follow-up from the student.

An encounter may span several lessons. On return, the teacher can use a saved example and their own notes to re-establish the idea without asking whether she studied it. If an optional question arrives, it can replace the planned topic.

### Encounter 1: “I can hear myself, and I can come back to this”

**Main intention:** reduce the distance between a musical gesture and keeping it in Live.

**Prepare:** one functioning instrument, a known output route, an empty place to record, and a named folder for this student's own work. Keep a teacher-created fallback phrase separate from her work.

**Opening, approximately 3 minutes:** open Live together, hear what she wants to try, and establish what is connected and audible. Let her try the sound. If useful, locate that immediate question on the Atlas map with one short label; no preliminary reading or territory tour is needed. Introduce the visible controls she actually needs. Setup may require more time; shrink the rest accordingly.

**Main exploration, approximately 22 minutes:** get from her key press to audible sound, then capture and hear a short musical thought if time permits. Distinguish the stored performance from the instrument through that example, without a full signal-flow explanation. Let her repeat the action. A metronome is optional; free-time playing is valid.

**Return point, approximately 5 minutes:** give the Set a recognizable name and save it in the appropriate Project. Locate the phrase or working setup together; reopen if practical. The teacher keeps a brief record of that saved point and enough context to restore it together next time, assuming no outside activity. A map location may accompany the note, but no marker or flag is required. Listening export can wait for another encounter.

**Natural stopping point:** a playable idea she can find again, plus enough orientation to stop and restart safely.

**If setup dominates:** a working sound and a documented way to return are enough. Capture an idea only if there is time; defer editing and the recording explanation as needed.

**If she moves quickly:** try another instrument with the same recorded phrase and discuss what stays the same. Do not add a catalog of synth types.

**Likely confusion:** selecting versus arming a track; MIDI activity without an instrument/output; hearing the physical keyboard and software instrument at once; recording in an unexpected location. Demonstrate one diagnostic check at a time.

**Teacher note, written during the work:** record the working setup, the phrase/Set location, and any useful distinction that came up. A few words are enough. She need not write, dictate, or complete anything.

**If an invitation is welcome:** “The notes are there if you want to return to this. We can also pick it up together next time.”

### Encounter 2: “Editing can serve the performance”

**Main intention:** hear editing as a choice rather than an obligation to correct the grid.

**Prepare:** the previous idea if she wants it, or a new short performance; a preserved original; a velocity-responsive instrument. Do not assume every preset changes timbre with velocity.

**Opening, approximately 3 minutes:** use the teacher's record to reopen together, listen first, and ask which moment feels most like what she meant. Assume nothing was explored between lessons; no recap reading is expected.

**Main exploration, approximately 22 minutes:** choose one dimension: a note, its onset, its duration, or its velocity. Preserve a version, change that dimension, and alternate with the original. Let her describe whether the result supports the phrase and try the operation herself. Other dimensions can become future lessons.

**Return point, approximately 5 minutes:** restore or choose the preferred version and save. Briefly revisit the operation and its way back. The teacher notes what changed and what she preferred; no written student reflection is requested.

**Natural stopping point:** she has made and evaluated one deliberate edit without losing the original.

**If overwhelmed:** use one note in one phrase; no full editor tour.

**If curious:** compare local timing adjustment with a modest quantization amount. Keep the original audible. There is no requirement that a quantized version win.

**Likely confusion:** velocity versus mixer volume; moving a note versus stretching it; note duration versus pedal or instrument release. Keep these separate and connect them when the sound demonstrates the difference.

**Teacher note:** a concrete comparison, her preference if expressed, and one useful term in plain language. A link to the relevant edit entry is optional.

**If an invitation is welcome:** “If you notice a detail you like in your playing, we can explore it next time.” No listening or editing task is set by default.

### Encounter 3: “The instrument changes the gesture”

**Main intention:** connect touch and phrasing to basic sound shaping.

**Prepare:** two edition-compatible sounds with clearly different responses, an original short phrase, and an untouched reference. Choose a simple envelope demonstration that actually sustains while a note is held. Media alternatives are acceptable if a device is unavailable.

**Opening, approximately 3 minutes:** the teacher recalls a sound from the previous lesson or offers a prepared contrast. A student question is welcome but not expected. Play before opening a parameter explanation.

**Main exploration, approximately 22 minutes:** hear the same notes through a quick-onset and a gradual-onset sound. Change attack, then let her alter her playing in response. Compare the stored phrase with a new performance for the changed sound. Introduce the word attack beside what she hears; release and the remaining envelope stages can wait.

**Return point, approximately 5 minutes:** keep and save a sound/phrase combination she likes. The teacher notes the audible change, the useful term, and where the example lives. Include the matching Atlas link for optional reference; do not require her to open it.

**Natural stopping point:** she can describe a sound's response and make one change that supports an intended gesture.

**If overwhelmed:** choose between two prepared examples and talk about the feeling. Parameter editing can wait.

**If curious:** introduce a small filter change or a velocity-sensitive response, not both together.

**Likely confusion:** release is not reverb; a sustained note does not guarantee a sustained sound; preset effects can obscure the envelope; sustain-pedal messages change the test. Isolate the example before generalizing.

**If an invitation is welcome:** “The sound-shaping page is there if this catches your interest. We can also explore another sound together next time.”

## 6. Five sample Atlas entries

These are original editorial drafts illustrating the voice and shape. Media descriptions are production briefs, not existing playback controls. Exact operations and screenshots must be checked in the student's setup before release.

### Touch changes more than volume

Concept: `edit-velocity`

**Musical question:** Can the same notes carry a different emphasis?

A MIDI performance can preserve how strongly each note was struck as a velocity value. That value is a message to the instrument. The instrument decides how to respond: it may change level, tone, attack, or several things together. Some sounds respond only slightly, or not at all.

This makes velocity different from turning the whole track up. One changes the information arriving with individual notes; the other changes the level of the resulting track signal. Both can affect emphasis, but they offer different kinds of control.

**Try, if you like:** preserve a short phrase, then choose one inner voice you would like to hear more clearly. Change the velocity of a few of its notes. Listen in the phrase, not only in isolation. Does it feel voiced differently, or merely louder? Try another sound if you are curious about the instrument's part in the result.

**A way back:** keep the untouched phrase nearby and undo or switch back when you want. No audible change may mean that this preset has little velocity response, not that the edit failed.

**Original demo to make:** one unaltered MIDI phrase, one with a deliberately emphasized inner voice, and the same pair on a contrasting instrument.

**Connections:** `play-instruments` for response; `edit-notes` for note-level decisions; `balance-levels` for emphasis after the sound is produced.

### Let a sound arrive and leave

Concept: `shape-envelopes`

**Musical question:** What happens if a chord enters gradually instead of speaking immediately?

A note and the sound it triggers are related, but they do not have identical shapes. An amplitude envelope controls how an instrument's level changes through a note's life. Attack shapes the onset; release shapes what happens after the note is released. Other envelope stages influence the sound while it continues.

Choose a simple sustaining sound for this first comparison. A recording that runs out, a short pluck, or a preset with heavy effects may behave differently from what this explanation suggests.

**Try, if you like:** hold a chord and compare a quick attack with a slower one. Then improvise a little with each setting. Does the slower arrival make you leave more space, anticipate the harmony, or choose different notes? Change release separately and listen to where one gesture ends and another begins.

**A way back:** restore the starting settings or return to the untouched reference. If the contrast is unclear, temporarily remove the sustain pedal and effects from the experiment. Short notes may end before a slow attack develops.

**Original demo to make:** a clearly labeled onset comparison, a separate release comparison, and a new phrase performed for the slower response.

**Connections:** `edit-notes` for note duration; `space-reverb-delay` for what happens around a sound; `move-modulation` for other kinds of change.

### Why am I hearing that clip?

Concept: `perform-views` · Canonical home: A, Live as an instrument and studio; connected to D's performance choices.

**Musical question:** I changed views. Why didn't playback change with me?

Session and Arrangement are two ways to organize material in one Live Set. Session is useful for choosing phrases and combinations as you go. Arrangement places events along a timeline. Switching the visible view does not itself choose the source of playback.

Launching a Session clip can take over its track from the Arrangement. Other tracks may still follow the timeline. Back to Arrangement restores timeline playback for the affected tracks; the view change alone does not. This is a small distinction with a large effect on what you hear. [Ableton's explanation of the two views and playback](https://www.ableton.com/en/live-manual/12/live-concepts/).

**Try, if you like:** with one clearly named track, listen to a phrase on the timeline, then launch an obvious variation from Session. Switch views and notice what continues. Restore Arrangement playback and compare again. Keep the experiment to one track until the relationship feels clear.

**A way back:** stop playback first if the result is confusing, then inspect the playing clip and the Back to Arrangement indication before restarting.

**Original demo to make:** one quiet, single-track Set with two unmistakable variations and a labeled screen recording of the change in playback source.

**Connections:** `perform-scenes` for combinations; `perform-capture` for recording choices; `keep-export` for checking what will actually be rendered.

### Make room before adding more

Concept: `build-subtract`

**Musical question:** Is something I like being hidden by something else?

When a passage feels crowded, the first useful change may be to the music itself. Two parts might speak at the same moment, occupy a similar register, or keep moving when a pause would let the phrase breathe. None of those situations automatically needs fixing. Start with what you want to hear more clearly.

An effect can help, but so can changing an entrance, shortening a note, lowering a layer, or leaving it out. The interesting comparison is not “processed versus unprocessed.” It is whether the change makes the relationship you want easier to hear.

**Try, if you like:** preserve a version of a passage with two parts. Choose one moment where attention feels divided. Temporarily mute the supporting part, then bring back only what you miss. You might prefer the original density. Listen to how the phrase behaves before and after that moment as well.

**A way back:** work in a duplicate or saved version, and use mute rather than deletion for the first comparison. Restore the original when the experiment stops being useful.

**Original demo to make:** one original two-part phrase compared with a changed entrance and with a shorter supporting gesture. Keep unrelated settings unchanged; do not present a universally winning version.

**Connections:** `build-roles` for what each part contributes; `balance-levels` for relative emphasis; `listen-compare` for hearing the actual consequence of a change.

### Keep an idea without promising it a future

Concept: `keep-fragments`

**Musical question:** I like this moment. How can I find it again without making it into a whole piece?

A useful idea can be smaller than a project: a phrase, a sound that responds well to your hands, or a combination you want to hear again. Keeping it does not mean adding it to a list of unfinished obligations.

Start with the simplest reliable method you already know: save the Set with a name you recognize. If useful, add a note saying where the moment is and what caught your ear. “Soft chord entrance near the end” may be a better reminder than a list of device settings. A listening copy can help you recognize it later, but it is optional.

**Try, if you like:** keep one thing from today's playing that you would enjoy meeting again. Give it a name that means something to you. Reopen it once to check that the return point works. That can be the whole activity.

**A way back:** save a separate version before reorganizing or reducing a Set. Do not delete other material just to make this idea look tidy. More compact ways of storing reusable material can wait until they are helpful.

**Original demo to make:** a short screen recording of saving an original phrase, adding a plain-language notebook reminder, and returning to the exact moment. No media upload or special library is required.

**Connections:** `keep-projects` for dependable saving; `keep-export` for a listening copy; `build-reuse` for encountering the idea in another setting.

## 7. Focused classes this system could support

These are future class concepts, not additional launch requirements or learner identities.

| Class | Core question | Concepts to reuse | Original class-specific work |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Expressive electronic instruments | How can an electronic instrument respond to the way I play? | `edit-velocity`, `shape-envelopes`, `move-macros` | Build a small, playable control setup; compare expressive gestures |
| A recording becomes an instrument | How can a captured sound suggest new music? | `sample-record`, `sample-play`, `sample-resample` | Follow one original recording through several transformations |
| Form through contrast | How do I develop an idea without endlessly adding parts? | `build-roles`, `build-contrast`, `build-form` | Compare density, silence, register, and order in an original sketch |
| A playable Live Set | What should be fixed, and what do I want to decide in the moment? | `perform-scenes`, `perform-capture`, `space-returns` | Design and rehearse a small performance with clear recovery actions |
| Mixing through listening | What prevents the musical intention from coming across? | `balance-levels`, `balance-eq`, `balance-dynamics` | Diagnose competing parts, try minimal changes, and compare fairly |
| One idea, several lives | How can familiar material become something new? | `build-reuse`, `sample-resample`, `shape-layers` | Rework one original gesture through changed context and sound while keeping its earlier forms |
| Making music together | How do we try different ideas without losing the shared intention? | `keep-projects`, `keep-portability`, `listen-compare` | Exchange an original sketch, preserve alternatives, and discuss a specific audible choice |

State practical entry skills and hardware needs for each class, without requiring a completed Atlas. A student may enter with equivalent experience from elsewhere.

Granular processing, generative systems, spectral analysis, advanced synthesis, and mastering remain possible later subjects. Their presence in the sources does not make them beginner requirements. Build each future class around a musical question and its actual tool requirements, not around replicating a source demonstration.

## 8. What to observe in the pilot

After teaching, privately note where she could act independently, where terminology got in the way, and which musical comparisons sparked a useful decision. Ask whether the between-lesson space feels inviting or demanding.

Evaluate the default case explicitly: could the half-hour work well with no advance reading or practice? Could you resume from your own brief notes? Did writing them interrupt attention or playing? Do not treat unopened resources or absent student annotations as lack of engagement. Any deeper exploration is a welcome addition, not evidence needed to validate the design.

When giving feedback, ask what she wants heard; point to a specific moment; describe what you hear before prescribing a change; try an alternative together if welcome; and let her decide what to keep. This is a teaching habit, not a mandatory student reflection form. A new question can be a better outcome than resolving everything at once.

If an invitation feels like homework, loosen or remove it. If an entry needs the teacher to translate every sentence, rewrite it. If writing notes takes attention away from the lesson, shorten them. Keep encounter and curiosity markers optional and separate from any claim of mastery; do not introduce automatic activity tracking.

The next content priority comes from real questions and the agreed musical goals, not from finishing the numbered map.
