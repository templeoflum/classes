## Recommended opening collection

### 1. Follow the sound — `hear-signal`

**Everyday question:** “The keyboard is connected. Why can’t I hear it?”

**Purpose:** provide a small model for locating an interruption: information arriving, an instrument producing sound, and audio reaching the chosen listening output. Distinguish MIDI playing from recording an instrument’s own audio.

**Home and connections:** A; `play-audio-midi` explains why incoming notes do not themselves make sound; `hear-monitoring` separates an interrupted route from an uncomfortable delay; `perform-views` addresses unexpected playback rather than silence.

**Lesson relevance:** immediately useful if setup needs attention. Showing the complete routing system is unnecessary. **Checks:** identify the actual connection, output, indicators, monitoring state, and safe recovery on the learner’s setup. Basic model checked against Ableton; no hardware test performed. **Essential future resource:** one reusable plain-language path plus a private setup note. A downloadable Set is not necessary.

### 2. Keep it and find it again — `keep-projects`

**Everyday question:** “How will we get back to this exact idea?”

**Purpose:** establish a recognizable saved return point and preserve an alternative before changing it; introduce Set, Project, and referenced media only as needed.

**Home and connections:** A; `keep-fragments` explains why saving need not promise a finished piece; `sample-play` makes the recording dependency concrete; `listen-compare` benefits from a preserved reference.

**Lesson relevance:** a small save/find/reopen action is useful in the first encounter; file portability can wait. **Checks:** installed edition permits saving, actual folder, Set/Project relationship, and reopening with its required media and devices. Saving a Set is not proof of a self-contained package. **Essential future resource:** a checked saving/returning note for the actual setup. Reuse the existing fragment sample’s emphasis on recognizable names and no obligation to finish; do not absorb or rename `keep-fragments`.

### 3. Let a sound arrive and leave — `shape-envelopes`

**Everyday question:** “Why does this sound make me want to play the phrase differently?”

**Purpose:** connect an instrument’s response through time with phrasing, preserving the difference between a played note and the sound it produces.

**Home and connections:** B, with D equally useful as an entry point; `edit-velocity` compares touch with time shape; `edit-notes` distinguishes note duration from sound duration; `hear-monitoring` distinguishes a gradual entrance from system delay.

**Lesson relevance:** an optional first encounter contrast if sound is already working; otherwise a rich later question. **Checks:** a sustaining instrument available in the chosen edition, the actual amplitude envelope, note-off/pedal behavior, and effects that obscure the example. **Essential future resource:** one original onset comparison; a release comparison can follow if needed. ### 4. A recording becomes playable — `sample-play`

**Everyday question:** “Could this recorded sound become an instrument?”

**Purpose:** distinguish the source recording, a selected portion, the way it plays, and a new performance made with it. Preserve the recognizable source as one valid choice alongside transformation.

**Home and connections:** B; `shape-envelopes` connects playback response with gesture; `time-loops` compares repeating a region with hearing a phrase; `keep-projects` explains why the source must remain available; the contextual reference below asks what repetition does in particular music.

**Lesson relevance:** optional breadth, or an early alternative if recorded sound is her stated interest. **Checks:** original recording provenance; supported device/mode; pitch, timing, looping, and envelope behavior of the chosen example. **Essential future resource:** one original source/result comparison. ### 5. A loop and a phrase — `time-loops`

**Everyday question:** “Why does this repeat feel settled here and interrupted there?”

**Purpose:** relate a software repeat boundary to a heard gesture. A loop’s selected span and a listener’s sense of phrasing need not coincide; changing the boundary can be a musical decision.

**Home and connections:** C; `sample-play` applies boundaries to recorded material; `perform-views` separates what repeats from what is currently playing; `build-subtract` asks what a pause contributes; the contextual reference explores repetition beyond a Live workflow.

**Lesson relevance:** optional unless repetition is already helping the lesson. Her phrasing knowledge is the bridge, with no basic-theory recap. **Checks:** distinguish clip loop from Arrangement loop; verify start/loop boundaries and the behavior of crossing notes and tails in the actual example. **Essential future resource:** one original phrase with two repeat spans and a readable description; no universal bar-count rule.

### Operational: Follow the sound — `hear-signal`

#### Student-facing draft

**The keyboard is connected. Why can’t I hear it?**

When you play a software instrument, your key press sends information, an instrument turns that information into sound, and an audio route carries the sound to your headphones or speakers. A flashing MIDI indicator shows activity; it does not prove that the instrument has received the notes or produced audio.

If this route goes quiet, keep the listening level modest and look for the first place where expected activity is missing. Does Live receive your playing? Is the intended track receiving it? Is an instrument present and able to sound? Does audio then reach the chosen output? Input selection, monitoring, and the track’s arm state can affect whether live playing reaches the instrument. A moving audio meter is a clue about that point in the route, not a guarantee that the speakers are connected to it.

This example concerns a software instrument. Recording a piano’s own sound follows an audio-input route; the distinction is explained in **Sound versus performance instructions** (`play-audio-midi`).

If you feel like exploring, follow this route while the sound is working. There is no need to create a fault. When a change becomes confusing, return to the known working settings recorded during the lesson before trying another change. Your teacher can keep that return point in the notes. Understanding the route is enough for now; nothing needs testing or recording after reading.

### Expressive sound: Let a sound arrive and leave — `shape-envelopes`

#### Student-facing draft

**What happens if a chord enters gradually instead of speaking immediately?**

A note and the sound it triggers can have different shapes. In a simple sustaining instrument, an amplitude envelope shapes how the level rises, continues, and falls. Attack controls the rise at the beginning; release controls the fall after the instrument responds to the note ending. These are parts of the instrument’s response, and that response can change what you want to play.

Imagine the same chord with an immediate entrance and then a gradual one. The second might invite a longer gesture, an earlier entrance, or more space before the next chord. None is an instruction to slow your music down. You might value the clear onset, enjoy the swelling sound, or find a place for both.

If this catches your interest, compare the two entrances on a simple sound, keeping the notes and other settings the same. Then let your hands answer the changed response. Comparing the same notes reveals what the instrument does; playing a new phrase reveals what it invites. Restore the starting settings whenever you want.

A short recording can run out before a long envelope finishes, and pedal or effects can obscure the comparison. **Note duration** (`edit-notes`) helps explain why releasing a key and hearing silence are different events; **reflections and echoes** (`space-reverb-delay`) considers sound continuing around the instrument. You can leave this as a listening idea. There is no patch to complete or experiment to bring back.

### Musical choice/listening: Several ways to make room — `build-subtract`

#### Student-facing draft

**Is something I like being hidden by something else?**

Imagine a melody arriving while a supporting chord is still sounding. You like both parts, yet the melody’s entrance does not draw your attention as you expected. That observation leaves several possibilities open. The chord could become quieter, end earlier, move to another register, or sit out for a moment. Each changes a different relationship: emphasis, overlap, spacing, or presence.

There may also be nothing to change. A crowded moment can have its own energy, and making every part equally clear may not be what the phrase needs. Start with what you hope to notice, then listen to what each alternative gives and takes away.

If you want to explore, preserve a two-part passage and temporarily silence the supporting part at one moment. What becomes audible, and what do you miss? Restore it, then try one different response, such as a quieter entrance. You can keep either version, keep both, or return to the original. A reversible mute and a preserved version give you a way back without deleting useful material.

**Compare two possibilities** (`listen-compare`) helps you return from the isolated moment to the surrounding phrase. **Let a sound arrive and leave** (`shape-envelopes`) offers another connection: the overlap may come partly from how long the instrument continues sounding. Reading this comparison is a complete use of the page. No passage needs fixing, and no choice needs reporting.

## Compact editorial pattern

Lead with a recognizable question and one relationship: a sound path, two interacting decisions, alternatives with different consequences, or a documented connection in musical practice. Explain the consequence before adding vocabulary. Use one original example or a carefully attributed listening reference; label imagined examples and unmade media in editorial records. Keep durable explanation separate from checked operation guidance.

Offer, rather than require, one exploration and an easy return. Give two or three connections with reasons. Make reading or doing nothing afterwards a complete use. Keep teacher facilitation, evidence, compatibility, and resource status outside student prose. The teacher may record a useful question in one sentence; no response form, homework link, role choice, or completion mark follows. Do not force historical context into every technical page or give every reference the same format as an operation.
