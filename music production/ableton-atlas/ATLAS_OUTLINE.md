# Ableton & Music Production Atlas

## Content outline

Draft 0.4 · September 20, 2026 · Proposed Atlas frame; viewpoints before surface design

An interconnected reference for hearing, making, shaping, and understanding music, with Ableton Live as the practical environment. The Atlas gives concepts, lesson notes, examples, and future demo projects a common home. It is broader than an introduction to Live, but it does not require a beginner to take on that breadth at once.

**This is an outline, not a finished course or a new interface build.** The existing local sketch remains a visual study; this pass changes the proposed content frame, not its surface. No demo Sets, audio, videos, or download packages are being made.

Read alongside the [teaching specification](</Users/donovan/work/classes/music production/ableton-atlas/SPEC.md>), [lesson plans and sample writing](</Users/donovan/work/classes/music production/ableton-atlas/CURRICULUM.md>), and [private source review](</Users/donovan/work/classes/music production/ableton-atlas/SOURCE_REVIEW.md>). This document now leads decisions about the Atlas's content organization. The earlier 42-entry map is a starting inventory, not a ceiling or a commitment to exactly 42 pages.

The [source index](</Users/donovan/work/classes/SOURCE_INDEX.md>) identifies the correct reference: [The Piano, Mapped](https://templeoflum.github.io/piano-atlas/), supplied by the user and now inspected. Draft 0.1 preceded that verification. This revision changes the organizing proposal; the fourteen-area list is retained as a coverage inventory, not treated as the finished Atlas structure.

## 1. What the Atlas must support

- A teacher and learner opening Live together and finding a useful explanation or example during a 30-minute lesson.
- A student revisiting something the teacher noted, if she wants to.
- Someone following a musical curiosity without knowing its technical name.
- A more experienced learner going deeper into a particular idea.
- Future focused classes drawing on the same concepts without duplicating them into separate courses.
- Choosing one useful demo project from its Atlas context and gradually keeping a personal collection.

The lesson remains the complete core experience. Teacher-written notes carry continuity. We assume no between-lesson reading, downloading, practicing, or note-writing. Vocabulary makes questions easier to express; it is never the admission price for asking them.

The first entry point accompanies the actual first opening of Live. Begin with the learner's situation—what she wants to try, what is connected, and what she can hear—and make the current question locatable on the map. Reading the Atlas is not preparation for opening the software. On return, the teacher brings back the saved working point and a brief reminder; no outside activity is needed to make that return work.

The outline describes places to explore, not a sequence to complete. Its numbering is for discussion and editing only.

## 2. Reference basis and limits

### Piano Atlas: confirmed basis, original adaptation

The structural review is recorded in section 11 of the [source review](</Users/donovan/work/classes/music production/ableton-atlas/SOURCE_REVIEW.md>). Neither the unrelated `pianoatlas.app` nor the chord viewer is the reference. The local checkout has not been located, but the public site and repository are verified.

Our adaptation should be a map of relationships and possibilities, not simply a course broken into small pages. Establish what belongs together, what changes, which approaches differ, and why a learner might care. The shape of each territory follows those relationships. A chronological story, a signal path, a listening comparison, and an explanation of competing choices are not interchangeable content structures.

The map is the primary way to explore. The initial layer shows recognizable places and labeled connections, not an interpretation of what the reader should hear, feel, or do. Definitions and deeper explanation are available when wanted. Spatial placement should communicate a specific relationship—such as signal flow, a contrast between choices, or a documented connection between practices. Proximity alone must not imply a historical influence or a required sequence. Each important relationship needs a readable label and optional explanation. We are not matching production topics one-to-one with the Piano Atlas's territories.

People plot their own paths through these relationships. A chosen trail can remember encounters or curiosities, but it is not a suggested route, an assignment sequence, or a measure of mastery. Any encounter or curiosity marker is deliberate and optional; opening pages or playing examples does not automatically record activity or infer progress. The teacher's lesson note remains the reliable return point.

Origins, people, aims, decisions, and disagreements are part of the map’s structure, not supplemental biographies. A technique connects to those who developed, used, reworked, or contested it and to what they sought under particular conditions. Show documented transmission, collaboration, convergence, and departure without implying that every relationship is ancestry. These routes coexist with mechanisms and musical relationships; they are not a compulsory history unit before touching Live. Do not invent a founder, influence, universal convention, or motive to complete a diagram. Each factual link needs evidence; editorial comparisons must be labeled as comparisons.

### Proposed Atlas frame: six views of one connected subject

These are viewpoints, not six stages, learner roles, or folders of mini-lessons. Each makes a different relationship visible. Names and boundaries are proposed for discussion; six is the result of this split, not a quota. This replaces the previous six-territory proposal without discarding its subject coverage.

| View | What it makes visible | Organizing relationship | Coverage inventory below |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Studio** | The working environment: inputs, tracks, clips, devices, views, outputs, Sets, and media | Where things live; what contains, controls, feeds, or plays what | Primarily 2 and 13; links to 3, 8, 9, and 12 |
| **Actions** | Finding, navigating, selecting, playing, recording, editing, comparing, recovering, and keeping work | An intention, the relevant object/focus, an operation, and its shortcut or gesture; reusable short action sequences | Operations throughout 2–13; a complete shortcut index extends beyond the earlier concept inventory |
| **Devices** | Every supported instrument, MIDI effect, audio effect, and rack, with its uses and connections | Signal type, mechanism, controls, related devices, and different applications | Primarily 6–9 and 11; a complete device index extends beyond the earlier concept inventory |
| **Sound & Form** | Relationships within a sound, among parts, and across a piece | Time, pitch, spectrum, dynamics, space, repetition, variation, and interaction at different scales | Primarily 1, 4–11; links to performance and recording |
| **Approaches** | What people seek in production and how they work toward it | Aims, conditions, choices, alternatives, and tradeoffs, anchored in particular practitioners and works | Practices across all areas, especially 3, 7, 8, 10–12, and 14 |
| **Lineages** | People, collaborators, communities, tools, works, and changing practices | Documented collaboration, transmission, influence, adaptation, disagreement, and independent convergence over time | Primarily 14, with sourced connections across the whole inventory |

**Why keep these distinctions?** Studio locates a device in a working system; Devices explains the device itself; Actions makes operating that system fluent. Sound & Form describes musical relationships without prescribing composition lessons. Approaches compares purposes and ways of working; Lineages traces actual historical relationships. A similar practice does not prove historical influence, and one practitioner can work in several ways.

The views need not look alike. Studio suggests connected objects and signal paths; Actions suggests context-sensitive families of movements and short sequences; Devices suggests related mechanisms and alternatives; Sound & Form suggests changing scale; Approaches suggests contextual comparisons; Lineages suggests branching networks and chronology. These are structural sketches, not six completed visualization designs. No forced binary spectrums, identical four-node stories, or universal person → aim → tool template.

People and their aims have substantial views of their own, as in the Piano Atlas's distinct frames. They are also reachable from relevant devices and concepts. This corrects the prototype's isolated historical mini-diagrams: a small biography attached to a tool is not a substitute for a view that makes relationships among people or approaches legible.

Keep distinctions such as output safety versus musical balance, a layered timbre versus several musical parts, and the visible Live view versus the material actually playing.

### Two complete references, encountered a little at a time

**Actions and shortcuts are core content, not an appendix.** Index every documented built-in shortcut for the supported Live version, including navigation, focus changes, modifier gestures, and recovery/file operations. Group by action and context, with both official names and ordinary-language search terms. Do not fabricate a shortcut for an action that has none; distinguish built-in keys from custom mappings and system/browser shortcuts.

Each shortcut entry needs the action, Mac/Windows keys, relevant selection/focus, version or context exceptions, resulting change, a way back where appropriate, and an official reference. A complete catalog does not mean memorizing it all or displaying it all at once. Keyboard navigation of the Atlas itself must remain accessible and distinct from the Live shortcuts it describes; the Atlas does not send commands to Live.

**Devices are equally complete within an explicit supported inventory.** Account for all core instruments, MIDI/audio effects, and racks, then enumerate Ableton-provided Max for Live and Pack devices with their dependencies. State the supported release, editions, and included Packs before claiming completeness. Third-party plug-ins and custom devices can extend the map; they cannot be silently included in an unbounded promise of “every device.” Catalog status and tested availability are separate authoring facts.

Each device entry needs what it receives and produces, its mechanism, useful controls, several possible uses, related/alternative devices, concepts, access steps, edition/Pack requirements, and verified official references. Uses are possibilities, not a single correct recipe. Demonstrations and individual demo downloads appear only when actually authored and checked. Do not promise a separate Set for every device or shortcut.

Coverage is checked against official [Live shortcuts](https://www.ableton.com/en/manual/live-keyboard-shortcuts/) and [device reference families](https://www.ableton.com/en/manual/working-with-instruments-and-effects/), not inferred from course handouts. Those references were consulted for scope and browser search behavior; neither complete catalog has been authored or hands-on tested yet. Explanations, diagrams, examples, and practice situations will be our own.

### First opening and the first learner

Start close in, alongside the actual first opening of Live: the relevant input, track/instrument, and output, with finding/loading and playing/hearing directly reachable. No atlas tour or task completion reveals the other views. Search, action lookup, and device lookup must be available without traversing the whole map.

The learner is a professionally trained musician and piano teacher with a strong ear and taste, as described by the teacher. Her central need is production vocabulary and software fluency: getting existing musical intentions into Live with less friction. Sound & Form remains an explorable reference, not remedial songwriting instruction. Recording, synthesis, routing, and processing can still be new even when composition is familiar.

The teacher reports *Headlock* by Imogen Heap and *Lost Weekend* by Phoebe Bridgers as her aspirational references. Keep them as personal reference points and ask about particular moments in the lesson. This frame does not claim a listening analysis, infer which production details she means, or turn either track into a reconstruction assignment. Student-specific preferences belong in private teaching context, not a public learner profile by default.

These two recordings and Live itself now anchor the initial Approaches/Lineages content. Map the songs’ actual credited people and materials, then documented decisions and origins; link the software’s musical history to the controls used today. Keep listening observations separate from production accounts and label our own Live study routes. Broad historical examples are not a substitute for these direct connections. See [reference research](</Users/donovan/work/classes/music production/ableton-atlas/REFERENCE_RESEARCH.md>) for evidence and open questions.

From the first lesson, practise useful movements in Live: need an action → recall or look up the shortcut → use it → reuse it when it naturally recurs. Browser search is an early example: select the intended track, use search, choose a result, and load it. Exact keys and focus behavior belong in version-checked action entries. A small musical task provides repetition; there are no speed tests, shortcut quizzes, or between-lesson requirements. The learner spends the practice time in Live, not in a simulated keyboard drill inside the Atlas.

### Shared access and personal continuity

- **Search:** concepts, ordinary descriptions, action names, shortcut chords, devices, people, and works; distinguish result kinds instead of returning an undifferentiated list.
- **Connections and return:** one stable entry can appear in several views. Switching view keeps the selected subject where a meaningful connection exists, otherwise offers an explicit related place rather than inventing a link. Temporary backtracking is not a permanent engagement record.
- **Lesson notes and kept places:** teacher-written notes, optional questions, and deliberate “return here” markers sit alongside all views. They are personal continuity, not a seventh subject view or a completion dashboard.
- **Examples and a growing collection:** a checked example or demo is reachable from its relevant entries and chosen individually. No bulk delivery, unlocks, default homework, or expectation of student activity.

Before another surface pass, test the frame with three cross-view anchors: finding/loading an instrument; keeping/editing a played phrase; and delay as a device/mechanism/musical relationship with a separately sourced context branch. Record which view adds a genuinely different relationship, where links are useful, and where a view has nothing to add. No obligation to populate all six views for every anchor.

### What connects the territories

- **Concepts and practices:** one reusable explanation with several relevant connections, not copies rewritten for each territory.
- **Examples and comparisons:** one original musical situation can illuminate several questions. State what changed and what stayed the same.
- **Context and tensions:** where an approach comes from, what it makes possible, what it gives up, and where another approach may fit. These are explanations, not debates the student must resolve.
- **Curiosities and lesson history:** an optional flag means “return to this”; an encounter marker can mean “we met this.” Neither claims understanding or mastery. The teacher's dated note remembers what happened, without a student-managed progress dashboard or automatic activity tracking.
- **Deeper resources:** an individually chosen demo, listening reference, or explanation belongs beside the question it helps explore. No homework sequence or automatic next-path recommendation follows it.

For the first student, a natural bridge is already musical: a familiar played phrase can lead into instrument response, timing, sound transformation, or keeping a recording. Which connection matters is decided together, not by an Atlas route.

## 3. Coverage inventory: fourteen connected areas

These areas retain the useful subject coverage from draft 0.1 and feed the proposed views above. They are an authoring index, not the top-level navigation or a fourteen-module course. The shortcut and device inventories are additional coverage obligations, not limited to these fourteen areas or the earlier 42 concepts. Each area contains a family of questions. Bullets are candidate coverage, not a promise that each phrase becomes its own page. Split an entry when it has a distinct question or needs a different example; combine material when a single explanation is clearer.

### 1. Listening, attention, and musical intention

**Starting question:** What am I hearing, and what do I want to hear differently?

- Notice foreground, background, density, space, energy, and changes in attention.
- Describe a sound in ordinary language, then connect that description to useful terms.
- Separate an observation, a preference, a technical problem, and a possible response.
- Compare two versions fairly; notice when a louder example is biasing the choice.
- Listen to a part alone and in context; return from the detail to the phrase or whole.
- Use a reference to ask a specific question without treating it as a recipe to reproduce.
- Keep a timestamp, a description, or a question rather than write a formal analysis.

**Vocabulary families:** level/loudness, register, density, texture, contrast, foreground/background, reference, comparison.

**Connections:** balance and mixing; arrangement; sound design; musical worlds and discovery.

**Possible original resources:** a short listening comparison; an annotated musical moment; the future project “What Should I Hear First.”

### 2. Getting oriented in Live and following the sound

**Starting question:** Where is the sound coming from, and how do I find what I need?

- The small working model: a Set, its tracks, clips, devices, and output.
- Finding a sound, playing/stopping, selecting the relevant material, and returning after a wrong turn.
- Audio versus MIDI activity; instrument and effect roles in a signal path.
- Input, track, group, return, and output as connected parts, introduced only when relevant.
- Arming, monitoring, mute/solo, and the difference between selecting something and hearing it.
- Comfortable listening levels, clipping, and a methodical response to silence or unexpected sound; relative musical prominence belongs in area 11.
- Responsiveness and monitoring delay on the actual equipment.
- Session and Arrangement as connected uses of one Set; the canonical `perform-views` playback explanation lives here and links to performed choices in area 12.

**Vocabulary families:** Set/Project, track, clip, device, input/output, arm, monitor, routing, meter, headroom, latency.

**Connections:** playing and recording; performance/playback; balance; dependable file care.

**Possible original resources:** “Hear Yourself”; a personal setup sheet; a small signal-flow explanation; a silence checklist. A demo file complements setup help but cannot solve hardware configuration by itself.

### 3. Playing, recording, and editing a performance

**Starting question:** How do I keep what I played and change only what I mean to change?

- A performed gesture, MIDI instructions, an instrument's response, and recorded audio.
- Capture an idea, listen back, preserve another take, and find it again.
- Choose a responsive instrument; compare how the same notes behave with different sounds.
- Edit pitch, onset, duration, or velocity deliberately and separately at first.
- Preserve touch and timing; distinguish an expressive detail from an unwanted event.
- Sustain pedal and other performance information when the example calls for them.
- Later depth: multiple takes, combining performances, expressive controllers, and relevant editing workflows.

**Vocabulary families:** take, note, velocity, duration, articulation, sustain, overdub, performance data.

**Connections:** rhythm/groove; musical ideas; envelopes; movement and control.

**Possible projects:** “Keep a Phrase”; “Sound vs Performance”; “Same Notes, Different Instrument”; “Touch and Emphasis”; “One Note Changes the Phrase.”

### 4. Time, rhythm, and groove

**Starting question:** What makes this move, settle, rush, or lean?

- Pulse, tempo, meter, subdivision, accent, and phrase: distinguish the different scales of time.
- Hear a grid as a reference, not a requirement that every event fall on it.
- Loop boundaries, start points, and the effect of repeating a particular span.
- Played timing, quantization choices, swing, and groove.
- Half-time and double-time perception; tempo labels versus the pulse being felt.
- Build a rhythmic relationship among parts, including silence and unequal emphasis.
- Audio timing: preserve a performance, align a specific event, or deliberately stretch it.
- Later depth: changing meter, overlapping cycles, polyrhythm, and more detailed timing transformations.

**Vocabulary families:** beat, bar, phrase, subdivision, downbeat, syncopation, swing, groove, quantize, warp.

**Connections:** performance editing; arrangement; sampling; scenes and launching.

**Possible projects:** “Timing with Character”; “Stretch a Moment”; an original phrase heard with several different rhythmic emphases. The source DJ material's common dance-music structures are examples, not universal meter or phrase rules.

### 5. Musical ideas, pitch, harmony, and phrasing

**Starting question:** What makes these notes feel like an idea, and what could that idea become?

- Gesture, motif, repetition, variation, and a phrase's direction.
- Pitch, intervals, tonal centers, chords, scales, and modes as needed by the question.
- Register, spacing, voicing, bass relationships, and movement between voices.
- Melody, accompaniment, ostinato, drone, and counterline as possible roles, not fixed templates.
- Tension, arrival, ambiguity, and contrast without declaring a single correct harmonic language.
- Translate a played idea into several instruments or layers while preserving what matters.
- Later depth: counterpoint, alternate tuning, and other compositional systems when wanted.

**Vocabulary families:** motif, contour, interval, voicing, register, tonal center, harmonic rhythm, tension/release.

**Connections:** expressive instruments; layers; arrangement; reference listening.

**Possible resources:** a phrase/voicing comparison and cross-links into “Two Voices” and “Familiar Idea, New Context.”

**Candidate entry boundary:** what makes a repeated span feel like a phrase? Connect pitch, rhythm, articulation, and expectation to `time-loops`, `build-reuse`, and instrument response. This is a proposed musical bridge, with topic support in Freshman Week 1 (S004; Source Review §4), not checked learner content or a new assigned ID. Voicing/register can first appear within a “make room” comparison; a separate entry needs its own useful question.

**First-student boundary:** this is optional musical reference, not a remedial theory unit for a piano teacher. Her existing knowledge may make this area a bridge into unfamiliar sound and production choices.

### 6. Instruments and sound design

**Starting question:** What kind of instrument or sound invites the gesture I want?

- Where a sound begins: recording, oscillator, noise, modeled behavior, or a combination.
- The relationship between pitch, spectrum, texture, and change through time.
- Attack, sustain, decay, and release in appropriate examples; connect response to playing.
- Filters and resonance as audible choices, not just curve shapes.
- Complementary layers within one sound: what each contributes in time, tone, or register; compare musical roles in area 10.
- Instruments, MIDI processing, and audio effects as different parts of a working setup.
- Later depth: subtractive, FM, wavetable, additive, and modeled approaches through specific sounds, not a required taxonomy tour.

**Vocabulary families:** source, oscillator, noise, harmonic, timbre, envelope, filter, resonance, layer, voice.

**Connections:** playing; movement; sampling; balance and distortion.

**Possible projects:** “Let the Sound Arrive”; “Let the Sound Leave”; “Bright to Soft”; “One Sound, Several Layers.”

### 7. Recorded sound, sampling, and transformation

**Starting question:** What can this recording become?

- Record original material and retain a recognizable source.
- Choose a useful region; trim, fade, reverse, loop, or slice for a musical purpose.
- Distinguish the recording from the way an instrument plays it.
- Make a sound playable through pitch, timing, envelopes, or mapping.
- Stretch time or change pitch with a specific listening intention.
- Resample a processed result and treat it as new material without losing the earlier state.
- Later depth: granular and spectral transformations, sound corpora, and generative methods with explicit dependencies.
- Track the origin and permitted use of material; possessing a recording does not automatically make it a distributable demo asset.

**Vocabulary families:** sample, region, transient, slice, loop, playback, stretch, resample, source.

**Connections:** rhythm/warping; sound design; reuse in arrangement; file dependencies.

**Possible projects:** “A Recording Becomes an Instrument”; “Stretch a Moment”; “Turn the Result into New Material.”

### 8. Movement, modulation, and expressive control

**Starting question:** What can change while the notes stay the same?

- Perform or draw a deliberate change through time.
- Automation versus other sources of movement, distinguished through hearing and behavior.
- Recurring motion, responsive motion, and controlled variation.
- Map a useful control, choose a sensible range, and understand what it affects.
- Combine several changes into one expressive gesture where that genuinely helps.
- Understand why a parameter is moving and how to regain control.
- Later depth: interacting modulators, probability, generative behavior, and expressive per-note controls where supported.

**Vocabulary families:** automation, modulation, envelope, LFO, mapping, macro, range, rate, depth.

**Connections:** instrument response; arrangement energy; performance; spatial effects.

**Possible projects:** “Movement Without New Notes”; “One Control, Several Changes.”

### 9. Space, depth, and stereo

**Starting question:** Where does the music seem to happen?

- Hear dry sound, reflections, and echoes separately before combining them.
- Distance, clarity, intimacy, and a shared sense of place.
- Delay as rhythm or a responding musical voice, not only an effect tail.
- Panning, width, and depth as related but different perceptions.
- Shared sends/returns and parallel paths when their purpose becomes audible.
- Mono listening and interactions between layers; phase relationships at greater depth.
- Later depth: modulation of space, spatial contrast across sections, and more complex routing.

**Vocabulary families:** dry/wet, reverb, delay, reflection, feedback, send, return, pan, width, mono/stereo.

**Connections:** phrasing; signal flow; movement; mix translation.

**Possible projects:** “Near, Far, and Somewhere Else”; “An Echo Answers”; a small stereo comparison if it adds a distinct question.

### 10. Developing and arranging an idea

**Starting question:** How can this become more meaningful without just adding more?

- Hear what each musical part contributes: lead, support, rhythm, texture, response, or contrast; compare constructing one layered sound in area 6.
- Repetition and variation; reuse familiar material in a changed context.
- Make room through subtraction, register, density, timing, or silence.
- Shape a beginning, a change, an ending, or a longer arc.
- Use provisional structure, markers, and rough versions to hear the whole.
- Connect energy, tension, texture, and expectation without assuming every piece needs a build or drop.
- Decide when detail supports the piece and when it hides the idea.
- Later depth: long-form cohesion, transitions, multiple sections, and deliberate constraints for a chosen project.

**Vocabulary families:** role, density, contrast, form, section, transition, motif, variation, negative space.

**Connections:** musical phrasing; listening; sound design; mixing; performed arrangements.

**Possible projects:** “Two Voices”; “Make Room”; “Familiar Idea, New Context”; “A Beginning, a Change, an Ending.”

### 11. Balance, mixing, and translation

**Starting question:** Is the musical intention coming across?

- Decide what should lead musically; distinguish that balance question from working output levels and clipping in area 2.
- Compare level, performance, register, density, and processing as different interventions.
- Hear EQ in context and avoid assuming that a spectrum display supplies the musical answer.
- Explore changing levels, transients, compression, and limiting with precise comparisons.
- Coloration, saturation, and distortion as intentional sound changes.
- Use grouping, parallel processing, or sidechains when a musical relationship calls for them.
- Listen at comfortable levels, take perspective, and compare in another listening context.
- Later depth: delivery checks and mastering as a separate purpose, not a mandatory chain on every sketch.

**Vocabulary families:** balance, gain, peak, headroom, masking, EQ, dynamics, transient, compression, saturation, reference.

**Connections:** listening and comparison; arrangement; sound design; space; exports.

**Possible projects:** “What Should I Hear First”; “Tone in Context”; “Changing the Shape of Dynamics.”

### 12. Performing and combining material in Live

**Starting question:** What do I want to decide while the music is happening?

- Use Session and Arrangement to perform choices; link to the canonical shared-track/playback explanation in area 2.
- Launch a phrase or combination deliberately; understand the timing of a launch.
- Scenes, clip relationships, loops, and variation in the moment.
- Record performed choices into a structure that can be revisited.
- Decide what stays prepared and what remains live.
- Keep stopping, recovering, and returning to a known state straightforward.
- Later depth: controller layouts, external instruments, other musicians, and reliable performance systems.

**Vocabulary families:** clip, scene, launch, global quantization, transport, Arrangement, mapping, monitoring.

**Connections:** rhythm; movement; arrangement; signal flow; dependable project preparation.

**Possible projects:** “Choose What Happens Next”; “Keep the Performance.” DJ Foundations contributes attention to preparation, listening, and recoverability, not a required two-deck format.

### 13. Keeping, finishing, and sharing work

**Starting question:** How do I preserve this, return to it, or let someone hear it?

- Save, name, locate, reopen, and preserve another version.
- Keep a phrase or useful sound without promising to finish a whole piece.
- Distinguish a Set, its Project, referenced media, and external device dependencies.
- Make a listening copy for a specific purpose; separate that from reusable project sharing.
- Organize a small collection of chosen Atlas projects and personal variations without requiring a library-management system.
- Exchange work and communicate intent while preserving alternatives.
- Choose a satisfying stopping point; revisit later when there is a reason.
- Later depth: collaboration packages, stems, delivery specifications, and finalization workflows.

**Vocabulary families:** Set, Project, version, backup, media dependency, export/render, stem, collection.

**Connections:** every area that makes or changes material; teacher lesson notes can identify a return point.

**Possible resources:** saving/returning guide; project-download instructions; one original export comparison if needed. Saving and versioning should also be practiced inside other demos rather than always require a separate Set.

### 14. Musical worlds, references, and discovery

**Starting question:** Where else can I hear or explore something like this?

- Start from a sound, rhythm, feeling, instrument, artist, or question rather than a fixed genre identity.
- Use styles and scenes as contextual starting points with porous boundaries.
- Connect an audible idea to particular music, performances, artists, labels, or collectives when there is a useful reason.
- Include acoustic, electronic, hybrid, song-based, dance, ambient, and experimental possibilities as the library grows; the first student's piano background is not a limit.
- Distinguish a listening reference, a technical explanation, an artist perspective, and a paid course.
- Learn where sounds and practices come from; do not reduce cultural histories to preset recipes or tempo ranges.
- Keep specific observations and bring them back to a lesson in any language.

**Vocabulary families:** style, scene, influence, reference, texture, groove, instrumentation, context.

**Connections:** all concept areas through a specific listening question, not indiscriminate recommendation lists.

**Possible resources:** small annotated listening selections, individual reference notes, and selected further-reading/watch links. DJ Foundations' existing genre cards and resource categories inform the structure; its factual histories, commercial links, and recommendations need fresh verification before reuse. Listening references do not require a download of third-party audio.

## 4. How entries connect

The same topic should not be rewritten in several places. Give each concept a stable home and link to it with a reason. A technical operation can support more than one musical intention.

| Everyday observation | Possible concept connections | The useful distinction |

## 5. The content unit: a concept with optional depth

Every entry starts with enough context to be useful on its own. The shared editorial core does not require every entry to have the same presentation or every field. It can contain:

1. A question or recognizable musical situation.
2. The main idea in plain language, with useful vocabulary beside it.
3. A short original example or comparison where hearing/seeing matters.
4. A practical foothold in Live, separate from the durable musical explanation.
5. An optional way to explore and a way back if the result is confusing.
6. A few related concepts, each with a reason.
7. An optional concept-linked demo project and a clear description of what it is for.
8. Selected deeper explanation or an external reference when it adds something specific.
9. Relevant origins, alternative approaches, or a tension that keeps the explanation from becoming a universal rule.
10. A question worth returning to, recordable by the teacher during a lesson or optionally flagged by the student.

Not every term needs a full entry. Not every entry needs a video or a project. A shared example may serve several concepts, with each entry explaining the relevant angle. Nothing is published as a finished download until it exists and has been tested.

The glossary is an index into these explanations, not a second competing textbook. Distinguish ambiguous words in context: a musical phrase versus a particular loop length; a note envelope versus an audio effect tail; a MIDI velocity value versus the level of the resulting sound.

### Three representative node outlines

These are original content sketches to test the new organization, not finished instructions or files.

**A familiar phrase, an unfamiliar response.** Start in Gesture/control, connect to Sound. Keep the same short original phrase while comparing two instrument responses, then let the player change her phrasing. The question is both what the instrument does and what it invites her to do. Connect velocity, note duration, envelopes, and listening; do not equate them. The tension is preserving a familiar gesture versus discovering one suited to the sound. Possible return question: “Why did I leave more space with the second sound?” Future individual project: “Same Notes, Different Instrument.” Source-pattern support: Freshman sound construction, Slam envelopes, and the user's musically fluent beginner context.

**A recording becomes material.** Start in Sound/transformation, connect to Live/studio and Musical worlds. Follow one original recording through keeping a source, selecting a moment, making it playable, and retaining a changed result. This is a branching set of possibilities, not four required steps. Connect sampling, phrase boundaries, instrument response, resampling, and file care. The tension is recognizability versus transformation; neither is the target by default. Possible return question: “How much of the original do I want to hear?” Future project: “A Recording Becomes an Instrument.” Source-pattern support: EMA reuse/transformation and Slam sampling/file care. Historical listening examples would be separately researched, not copied from course assets.

**Several ways to make room.** Start in Listening/perspective, connect to Musical ideas/form and Sound. Use an original two-part phrase and compare changing a part's level, register, duration, or presence. The purpose is to hear different kinds of response to the same observation, not teach a mandatory order of fixes. Connect roles, voicing, subtraction, balance, and optional processing depth. Possible return question: “What do I want to notice first?” Future project: “Make Room.” Source-pattern support: Freshman/Thesis contextual decisions and Synthesis bounded comparison.

In any of these, the teacher can write one sentence and stop. The student may follow a connection, flag a curiosity, choose a project, or never open the Atlas. No outcome depends on those optional actions.

## 6. The demo-project collection

The intended experience is to encounter a concept, choose its project if interested, and keep a useful experiment. Downloading is optional, and the teacher can use the same demo during a lesson without asking the student to prepare it first.

- Projects are obtained individually from the Atlas entries that explain them. No default download-everything package.
- “One at a time” means deliberate individual choices, not a rate limit, timed release, or achievement unlock. Any available concept can be explored whenever wanted.
- A project has one clear primary question; related entries can link to it without duplicating the package.
- The project describes its concept, a starting action, an optional variation, and how to preserve or restore the starting material.
- When produced, each package will identify its tested Live version/edition, dependencies, original assets, and release version before download.
- The student may keep the original and save personal variations under meaningful names. A growing local collection is enough; no tracking or collection score is required.
- A brief audio/text alternative can make the concept accessible without downloading or opening a Set.

The previously sketched project titles are candidate examples attached to this outline, not files that exist and not a competing syllabus. Choose which to author after the content boundaries are reviewed. Content leads the file list.
